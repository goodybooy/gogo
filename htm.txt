<header class="masthead">
  <p class="eyebrow">Research note · portfolio construction</p>
  <h1>Estimating the transfer coefficient<em>for a Merton-based credit book</em></h1>
  <p class="standfirst">How much of a bottom-up default-risk signal actually survives the constraints you impose on the portfolio — and which constraint is doing the damage.</p>
</header>

<div class="slab">
  <p class="label">Definition</p>
  $$\text{TC} = \text{Corr}\big(w^{\text{actual}},\ w^{\text{ideal}}\big), \qquad w^{\text{ideal}} = \Sigma^{-1}\alpha$$
  <p class="gloss">
    <b>Cross-sectional</b> correlation across names on a single date, then averaged over dates — not a time-series correlation.<br>
    Enters the Fundamental Law as a straight haircut: <b>IR = IC × √BR × TC</b>.
  </p>
</div>

<section>
  <p class="lede">The mechanics are signal-agnostic: build both portfolios on the same date with the same inputs, and correlate the weight vectors. What makes it specific to a Merton book is how you get to \(\alpha\) and \(\Sigma\).</p>
</section>

<div class="step">
  <span class="n">01</span>
  <div>
    <h3>Convert the residual into return units</h3>
    <p>The signal is a residual in log-spread space. It has to become an expected return before it can be optimised against a variance term.</p>
    $$\alpha_i = \text{IC} \times \text{SD}_i \times \text{OAS}_i \times \varepsilon_i^{z}$$
    <p>The \(\text{SD}_i \times \text{OAS}_i\) term carries more weight here than it would in FX. A 20% cheapness residual is worth far more on a long-duration, high-spread bond than on a short-dated investment-grade name, because the same proportional tightening produces more price return. Skip it and the "ideal" portfolio is wrong before the comparison starts.</p>
  </div>
</div>

<div class="step">
  <span class="n">02</span>
  <div>
    <h3>Use a factor covariance, not the sample matrix</h3>
    <p>With hundreds of bonds and limited clean price history, sample covariance is rank-deficient and \(\Sigma^{-1}\) does not exist in any useful sense.</p>
    $$\Sigma = B\Omega B' + D$$
    <p>Load \(B\) with key rate durations, spread duration × sector, and rating bucket. This is what makes the inversion tractable at all, and it is the same structure a commercial multi-asset risk model uses for exactly this reason.</p>
  </div>
</div>

<div class="step">
  <span class="n">03</span>
  <div>
    <h3>Solve twice, correlate once</h3>
    <p>Run the optimiser unconstrained, then with the full constraint set, holding \(\alpha\) and \(\Sigma\) fixed. Correlate the two weight vectors. Repeat each rebalance date and average.</p>
  </div>
</div>

<hr>

<h2>The single number tells you almost nothing</h2>

<p>A TC of 0.42 is not actionable. What is actionable is the marginal cost of each constraint — so add them one at a time and watch where the drop happens.</p>

<hr>

<h2>Long-only will dominate everything else</h2>

<p>If cash bonds cannot be shorted, that one constraint sets the transfer coefficient and the rest is noise around it. Long-only credit typically runs 0.3–0.5, because a Merton residual is symmetric: roughly half the names are rich, and the only way to express that view is an underweight bounded at zero. Half the information is discarded on arrival.</p>

<h3>Check which half is worth more</h3>

<p>Compute IC separately on the cheap and rich halves of the cross-section. Rich names often predict better — cheapness can persist for structural reasons the model does not see, while expensiveness mean-reverts more reliably. If that holds in your data, long-only is discarding the stronger half.</p>

<div class="note">
  <span class="tag">Why this is the argument for CDS</span>
  <p>Single-name CDS shorts cleanly. If the ablation shows long-only costing 0.5 while every other constraint costs 0.05 combined, that number is the case for the instrument — and it is far more compelling than "CDS is cheaper to trade." It is also the reason a systematic credit book built on structural default probabilities ends up in CDS rather than cash.</p>
</div>

<hr>

<h2>One caveat on reading the number</h2>

<div class="flag">
  <span class="tag">TC assumes the ideal is ideal</span>
  <p>If \(\Sigma\) is ill-conditioned — likely with correlated bonds and a thin factor model — the unconstrained optimum is error-maximised, and a <em>low</em> TC means the constraints are protecting you rather than costing you.</p>
  <p>Report the condition number \(\lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}\) alongside. A high TC on a badly conditioned \(\Sigma\) means faithfully implementing a portfolio driven by the least reliable eigenvalue, which is worse than a low TC.</p>
</div>

<p class="closer">Transfer coefficient is not a scorecard. It is a map of which constraints are expensive, and the ablation is where the information lives.</p>
