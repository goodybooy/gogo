Convergence Trades: Half-Life, Carry, and the Error-Correction Model

Two connected questions — what carry a 180-day mean-reversion trade must earn to justify its capital, and why the cointegrating β is the thing that makes the spread tradeable at all.

Notes · 2026-08-05

Part I — What carry does a 180-day half-life demand?

The question as asked was underspecified

Asked as written, there is no unique number. "Minimum carry" depends on three inputs the question doesn't supply: the current dislocation size, the spread volatility, and your return hurdle. What follows is the framework that produces an answer, then a defensible one.

The actual trade-off

You aren't really asking "what carry do I need." You're asking does convergence beat the cost of waiting? The condition is:

E[convergence gain over horizon] Signal > carry cost × horizon Bleed

Expected convergence at horizon h, from the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck dynamics:

E[gain] = D0(1 − e−θh), θ = ln 2t1/2

With t1/2 = 180 days: θ = 0.693 / 180 = 0.00385 per day, or θ ≈ 1.4 per year. At h = 1 year you capture 1 − e−1.4 = 75% of the initial dislocation D0.

Break-even carry

c* = D0(1 − e−θh)h at h = 1 yr:  c* = 0.75 × D0 per year

Plugging in realistic numbers

Say the spread is dislocated D0 = 20 bp — a typical entry signal, roughly 1.5–2σ on a cross-country swap spread.

c* = 0.75 × 20 bp = 15 bp / year

So carry cost must stay under roughly 15 bp/yr just to break even. Anything above that and the position bleeds faster than it converges.

But break-even isn't the bar

Demand a Sharpe of 0.5+ on the trade. With spread vol σ ≈ 25 bp/yr (typical for USD–EUR 10y) and expected 1yr P&L of 15 bp − c:

SR = 15 − c25  ≥ 0.5 ⇒ c ≤ 2.5 bp/yr

That is brutal, and it's the real answer: at a 180-day half-life you need the position to be nearly carry-neutral. In practice you want it positively carried — the trade should pay you to wait.

The deeper problem is statistical, not arithmetic

With a 180-day half-life you observe roughly T/180 independent convergence episodes. Over five years of daily data that is about seven effective observations. Your θ̂ carries enormous standard error, your ADF was borderline by construction, and you cannot distinguish t1/2 = 180 from t1/2 = ∞ with any confidence.

You're betting real capital on a parameter you haven't actually measured.

And the horizon itself exposes you to exactly what breaks these trades: over six to twelve months, central bank paths diverge and the "equilibrium" you are reverting to relocates.

The verdict

Don't put it on as a pure convergence trade. At t1/2 = 180 days, either:

  1. Require positive carry — then the spread is optionality, not the thesis. You get paid to wait and convergence is upside.
  2. Size it as a small satellite with a hard time stop (exit at one half-life regardless) and demand a wider entry — 2.5σ+ rather than 1.5σ.
  3. Find a faster leg. The same trade in 2y instead of 10y, or a butterfly that strips out the common global factor, usually reverts faster because it isolates a more local dislocation.
Rule worth memorizing

Your half-life should sit well inside your funding-certainty horizon. If you can't guarantee holding for two to three half-lives through a drawdown, the reversion never gets a chance to pay you.

Part II — Cointegration, β, and error correction

Case A, restated

Both yt and xt are I(1), but yt − βxt is I(0). There is a genuine long-run equilibrium; deviations are temporary and revert. The level relationship is real, and differencing destroys the information you care about — you've thrown away the error-correction term.

The refinement: you don't have to choose

The instinct is right — take Δy against Δx and the error-correction term is gone; use y − βx and it's preserved. But the correct model uses both, and that is exactly what an ECM is.

Δyt = α +. λ(yt−1 − βxt−1) Long run — the spread  + . Σi φi Δxt−i Short run — the changes  + εt.

Every term in the ECM is stationary, so standard t-statistics and standard errors are valid again. That's the technical payoff: no spurious-regression problem, no unit-root critical-value tables.

Why β is doing something special

Calling β "the connection" is right, but it's worth being precise about why it is not an ordinary regression coefficient.

Super-consistency

When y and x are cointegrated, β̂ from OLS converges at rate T, not √T. In a normal regression, doubling your sample cuts the standard error by √2; here it cuts it by 2.

The intuition: OLS minimizes residual variance, and there is exactly one β that makes the residual I(0) — every other value leaves an I(1) residual whose variance grows in T. So the objective function has an enormously sharp minimum. The estimator is dragged to the true value fast, and it stays consistent even under endogeneity or omitted dynamics.

β is the only value that works

This is the deep point. Cointegration says a specific linear combination is stationary — not any combination. Get β wrong by 10% and your "spread" is still I(1) and still non-tradeable.

Practical consequence

Hedge-ratio estimation is the whole ballgame in pairs trading. Using a naive 1:1 or DV01-matched ratio when the true β ≠ 1 silently destroys the trade.

Caveat on inference

Super-consistency means the point estimate is reliable, but the OLS standard error on β̂ is not — its distribution is non-standard. Use FM-OLS (Phillips–Hansen), DOLS (Stock–Watson), or Johansen if you need inference on β itself.

The Granger Representation Theorem

This is the formal statement of the intuition above, and it's the thing to be able to cite:

yt, xt are cointegrated ⇔ an error-correction representation exists

The equivalence runs both ways, which is stronger than it looks. Cointegration guarantees at least one variable error-corrects — the system cannot drift apart indefinitely without something pulling it back. And the existence of an ECM implies cointegration.

Reading λ

Same interpretation as the γ in an ADF regression — it's the same spring constant.

λMeaning
λ < 0Required. y falls when the spread is too wide.
λ = 0No error correction — no cointegration.
λ = −0.055% of the gap closes per day; t1/2 = 13.5 days.
λ < −1Overshooting, oscillatory correction.

Note that testing λ = 0 in the ECM is itself a cointegration test (the Banerjee/ECM test), and it often has better power than the two-step Engle–Granger procedure.

The trading payoff

For the USD/EUR swap case, run the ECM in both directions:

Δyt = λy(spreadt−1) + … Δxt = λx(spreadt−1) + …

Which λ is significant tells you which leg does the adjusting — that is, where price discovery lives, and which leg to trade.

PatternInterpretation
λy sig., λx ≈ 0 x is the anchor and y corrects toward it. Trade y; x is weakly exogenous.
Both significant Both adjust — trade the spread as a package.
Neither significant You don't have cointegration, whatever the ADF said.

This is the Hasbrouck / Gonzalo–Granger price-discovery framework, and it's genuinely useful — for cross-listed assets or cash-versus-futures it identifies which venue leads.

The signal, in one term

λ × spreadt−1 is your expected next-period P&L. Position size ∝ that quantity, capped by spread volatility. That is the entire stat-arb signal.
